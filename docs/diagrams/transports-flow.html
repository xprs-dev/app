How a packet finds a radio

Every XPRS packet leaves through one fan-out and arrives through one funnel. These are the decisions each of them makes — and the branch that was missing, which meant a station carried mail for nobody on any radio.

FIG 1 Where a packet enters and leaves

Four bearers, two directions. The subtype matters more than it looks: XPRS airs on 0x58, and the custody tap that used to own carrying was wired to 0x41.

FIG 2 Sending a 1:1

§36.0 permits choosing one path when there is recent evidence of several to the same station. With no evidence it fans out — that is the same section's own fallback, not a shortcut.

FIG 3 What one bearer does with one wire

Four verdicts, because they mean four different things to whoever reads the report.

sentOn the air, or acknowledged by the medium.
queuedHanded to a lane that stores and forwards. Outcome not known yet.
refusedThe medium said no to this frame.
disabledSwitched off by the operator or a test — distinct from a radio being off.
why queued had to exist

Measured on the bench: a directed wire reported reticulum: refused and arrived at the far station over Reticulum seconds later. That bearer hands the packet to two lanes and could only report one. A report that calls an arriving packet refused is worse than no report — answering “where did this actually go” is the whole job.

FIG 4 Demux, then the funnel

One owner per form. An XPRS packet goes to the funnel whatever subtype carried it; the legacy compact frame goes to the custody tap, which is the only thing that understands it. Both used to run for every frame.

FIG 7 Taking a lane away, on purpose

None of the fallback behaviour is observable while four healthy bearers are up — which on a bench they always are. So the bearers can be switched off at runtime.
